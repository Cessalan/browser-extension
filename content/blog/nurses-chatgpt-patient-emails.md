Nurses, case managers and care coordinators write a lot: discharge follow-ups, transfer requests, messages to families and physicians. AI assistants make that writing faster and clearer, and many clinicians already use them.

The catch is what happens to the text.

## What happens when you paste an email into ChatGPT

Online assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude run on the provider’s servers. When you paste an email:

- the text **leaves your computer** and is processed by a third party;
- depending on the account and settings, it may be **kept** for a while;
- a personal or free account usually comes with **no agreement** between your organization and the provider.

In a widely cited JAMA commentary, physicians warned that health systems whose providers use ChatGPT carelessly with patient data could be exposing themselves to HIPAA violations ([Becker’s Hospital Review](https://beckershospitalreview.com/cybersecurity/the-potential-healthcare-privacy-risks-of-chatgpt.html)). Outside the US, privacy laws such as Quebec’s Law 25 raise similar questions.

This article isn’t legal advice: follow your organization’s policy, and ask your privacy officer when in doubt.

## “I’ll just remove the name”

De-identifying text by hand is harder than it looks. The same commentary lists what has to go: names and nicknames, small geographic details, and admission and discharge dates. A room number, a rare condition or an unusual situation can still point to one person. And when you’re busy, it’s easy to miss a medical record number at the bottom of a message.

## Good habits, whatever the tool

1. **Include the minimum**: a record number rather than a full name and date of birth.
2. **Review before sending**: AI can make mistakes, even on your device.
3. **Use secure channels** for sensitive details when your organization provides them.
